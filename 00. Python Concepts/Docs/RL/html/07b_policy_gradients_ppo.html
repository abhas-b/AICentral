Part 7b — Deep RL II: policy gradients, PPO and Stable-Baselines3¶

DQN learns values and derives a policy ("pick the highest Q"). Policy-gradient methods learn the policy directly: a network that outputs a probability for each strategy.

Analogy.

  • Value-based (DQN): a food critic who scores every dish, then orders the top-scored one.
  • Policy-based (PPO): a regular who never writes down scores, but after each meal nudges their habits: "that was good, I'll order it a bit more often in this kind of mood."

Why bother, when DQN works?

  • Policy methods handle stochastic policies naturally. Mixing between two near-equal strategies can beat always picking one noisily.
  • They extend to continuous actions (e.g. "allocate 30% of risk to the condor, 70% to the bull put"), where "max over actions" is impossible.
  • PPO is the robust, general-purpose workhorse of modern deep RL.

Plan: (1) the policy-gradient idea with a tiny REINFORCE you write yourself, (2) PPO's key idea, the clipped objective, (3) PPO and DQN from Stable-Baselines3 with proper validation-based model selection, (4) an honest comparison.

7.4 The policy-gradient idea¶

The policy is a network with a softmax output: $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$. We want to increase the expected reward $J(\theta)$. The policy-gradient theorem says:

$$ \nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\big[\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \cdot A(s,a) \,\big] $$

In words: for each action you took, push up its log-probability in proportion to how much better than expected it turned out, the advantage A. Better than expected → more likely next time. Worse → less likely. Subtracting a baseline (what you expected) to get A instead of using raw reward doesn't change the direction on average, but it massively reduces noise. That matters a lot with P&L this noisy.

Worked example. Three strategies with logits [0, 0, 0] → π = [⅓, ⅓, ⅓]. You picked strategy 2 and it beat expectations: A = +0.6. For a softmax, ∇ log π(2) w.r.t. the logits is (one-hot(2) − π) = [−⅓, −⅓, +⅔]. With learning rate 0.5: logits += 0.5 · 0.6 · [−⅓, −⅓, ⅔] = [−0.1, −0.1, +0.2] → π ≈ [0.30, 0.30, 0.40]. One good outcome made strategy 2 a bit more likely. It isn't certain, just more likely.

🛠 Mini-project 7.4: REINFORCE from scratch on the weekly selection problem¶

Train a policy network (7 features → 32 → 8 logits) on the contextual-bandit version of the problem (one week = one episode, so the return is just that week's reward). Each update:

  1. sample a batch of 64 training weeks;
  2. sample an action per week from π;
  3. advantage = reward − baseline (a running average of rewards);
  4. loss = −mean(log π(a|s) · advantage) − β · entropy (the entropy bonus keeps it exploring).

Evaluate the greedy policy (most likely action) on the test weeks.

Expected: the policy learns quickly and on test earns roughly what the best single strategy does or somewhat more (≈ $50–85/week depending on the seed). Its training score is far higher, the same overfitting story as DQN.

def reinforce(iters=1500, batch=64, lr=3e-3, ent_coef=0.02, seed=0):
    torch.manual_seed(seed); rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    pi = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(7, 32), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(32, 8))
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(pi.parameters(), lr=lr)
    baseline, curve = 0.0, []
    for it in range(iters):
        idx = torch.from_numpy(rng.integers(0, cut, batch))
        dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=pi(Xt[idx]))
        a = dist.sample()
        r = Rt[idx, a]
        adv = r - baseline
        baseline = 0.95 * baseline + 0.05 * r.mean().item()
        loss = -(dist.log_prob(a) * adv).mean() - ent_coef * dist.entropy().mean()
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
        curve.append(r.mean().item())
    return pi, curve

7.5 PPO: policy gradients with a safety rail¶

Plain REINFORCE has a flaw: one lucky batch (a crash week that made the long straddle look brilliant) can produce a huge update that wrecks a good policy. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization, Schulman et al. 2017) adds two things.

1. An actor–critic setup. A second network head, the critic, learns V(s). Advantages are computed as "what actually happened" minus V(s), using GAE (generalised advantage estimation, a smooth mix of 1-step and multi-step returns). A much better baseline means much less noise.

2. The clipped objective. Let $\rho = \pi_{\text{new}}(a|s) / \pi_{\text{old}}(a|s)$ be how much the update changes an action's probability. PPO maximises

$$ \min\big(\rho\,A,\;\; \text{clip}(\rho,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,A\big) $$

In words: improve the policy, but once an action's probability has moved more than ±ε (typically 20%) from where it was, stop pushing. Take small, safe steps and re-evaluate.

Trading analogy. A risk committee that lets you tilt your strategy mix after a good month, but caps any single change at ±20% until more evidence comes in.

Worked example (ε = 0.2). Good action, A = +1:

  • ρ = 1.1 → min(1.1, 1.1) = 1.1: still improving, keep pushing.
  • ρ = 1.5 → min(1.5, 1.2) = 1.2: clipped, so there's no gain from pushing further. The gradient is 0.

Bad action, A = −1: ρ = 0.5 → min(−0.5, −0.8) = −0.8, clipped at 1 − ε. Once the bad action's probability has been cut by 20%, PPO stops cutting it further on this batch.

def ppo_objective(rho, A, eps=0.2):
    return np.minimum(rho * A, np.clip(rho, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * A)

for rho, adv in [(1.1, 1), (1.5, 1), (0.5, -1), (0.9, -1)]:
    print(f"ρ={rho}, A={adv:+d} → objective {ppo_objective(rho, adv):+.2f}")

7.6 Stable-Baselines3: PPO and DQN on the trading environment¶

In practice you don't hand-write PPO. Stable-Baselines3 (SB3) gives tested implementations that plug into any Gymnasium env (Part 6).

Doing it properly: validation-based model selection. We learned in 7a that training longer can hurt. So we split the training years again:

period used for
2006 – mid-2016 (fit) gradient updates
mid-2016 – 2019 (validation) picking which checkpoint to keep (EvalCallback)
2019 – 2025 (test) the final, one-time evaluation

Never pick models or hyperparameters on the test period. Every peek makes it less "unseen".

In [6]:
from stable_baselines3 import PPO, DQN
from stable_baselines3.common.env_util import make_vec_env
from stable_baselines3.common.callbacks import EvalCallback
from stable_baselines3.common.monitor import Monitor

fit_mkt, val_mkt = train.split(0.8)
print("fit:", fit_mkt.df.index[0].date(), "→", fit_mkt.df.index[-1].date(),
      "| validation:", val_mkt.df.index[0].date(), "→", val_mkt.df.index[-1].date())

def make_fit_env(seed=0):
    return optrl.OptionsSelectionEnv(fit_mkt, obs_stats=stats, seed=seed)

def train_sb3(algo, total_steps, seed=0, eval_freq=5_000, **kw):
    vec = make_vec_env(lambda: make_fit_env(seed), n_envs=4 if algo is PPO else 1, seed=seed)
    val_env = Monitor(optrl.OptionsSelectionEnv(val_mkt, obs_stats=stats, episode_len=26, seed=seed + 100))
    cb = EvalCallback(val_env, n_eval_episodes=20, eval_freq=eval_freq // (4 if algo is PPO else 1),
                      deterministic=True, best_model_save_path=f"models/{algo.__name__}_s{seed}",
                      log_path=f"models/{algo.__name__}_s{seed}", verbose=0)
    model = algo("MlpPolicy", vec, seed=seed, verbose=0, **kw)
    t0 = time.time(); model.learn(total_steps, callback=cb)
    best = algo.load(f"models/{algo.__name__}_s{seed}/best_model")
    return best, model, cb, time.time() - t0

ppo_kw = dict(n_steps=256, batch_size=256, n_epochs=10, learning_rate=3e-4, gamma=0.9, gae_lambda=0.9,
              clip_range=0.2, ent_coef=0.01, policy_kwargs=dict(net_arch=[64, 64]))

How to read this honestly.

  • Deep RL agents can beat the fixed strategies on average P&L, but often with higher costs and deeper drawdowns. Whether they beat them on a risk-adjusted basis is the real question. Look at the Sharpe and drawdown columns, not just total P&L.
  • Look at which strategies the agents use (heatmaps). With reward = raw P&L, both gravitate to the debit spreads (bull call / bear put): large, lopsided payoffs that maximise average P&L when the direction call is right, at the price of big swings and a low win rate. That's what "maximise expected P&L" means literally. Exercise 7.7a changes the reward and watches the behaviour change.
  • The "last checkpoint" vs "best validation checkpoint" rows show whether early stopping helped on this test period. It often does, though not always. It's a sound procedure, not a guarantee.
  • This is one test period and one training seed. You can't yet tell skill from luck. Part 8 is devoted to exactly that.

Recap¶

  • Policy gradient: push up log π(a|s) in proportion to the advantage. Baselines cut noise.
  • PPO = actor-critic + a clipped objective (small, safe policy updates) + an entropy bonus.
  • SB3 gives you PPO/DQN in a few lines. Your job is the environment, the evaluation protocol and the judgement.
  • Use a validation period + EvalCallback to pick checkpoints. Never tune on the test period.
  • Single runs are anecdotes: seeds matter.
